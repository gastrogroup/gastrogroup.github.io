Colonoscopy Pre-Procedure Instructions

Please follow the instructions below prior to your colonoscopy. Also be sure to follow the instructions for your specific bowel preparation prescribed by your physician — select your prep below.

Diabetes or Blood Thinning Medications: Please contact the provider who manages these medications before your procedure and ask whether you should stop taking them and for how long prior. Please discuss blood thinner use with your physician prior to discontinuation.

These eating and drinking instructions apply to ALL bowel preparations.

  1. 1
    2 Days Before Your Procedure
    Do not eat foods high in fiber. Examples: whole grain breads and cereals, fruits, nuts, seeds, quinoa, popcorn, and vegetables (cooked or raw).
  2. 2
    1 Day Before Your Procedure — Clear Liquid Diet Only
    Do not eat solid foods. Drink only clear liquids: water, clear fat-free broth, gelatin, clear fruit juice (apple or white grape), ice pops, carbonated beverages, sports drinks, tea, and coffee (sugar okay). Do not eat or drink dairy products.
  3. 3
    Day of Your Procedure
    Do not eat solid foods.
  4. 4
    Medications
    If you take daily medications, you may take them with sips of water before your scheduled arrival time. If you have diabetes or take blood thinners, follow instructions from the provider who manages those medications.
  5. 5
    Transportation
    Make arrangements for a responsible adult to drive you home. You may NOT leave in a taxi, rideshare, or other transportation service without a responsible adult accompanying you.
Is my prep working? Your prep is complete when your stool output is clear yellow or pale — similar to apple juice with no solid material. If you are still passing brown or cloudy material, contact our office.
Do NOT take iron for one week prior to your procedure. Notify our office if you are taking any blood thinners or GLP-1 agonists (diabetes/weight loss injectable medications such as Ozempic, Wegovy, Mounjaro).

Breath Testing — FoodMarble AIRE
  1. 1
    48 hours before: Avoid high-fiber foods, fermented foods (yogurt, kefir, sauerkraut, kombucha), and probiotics.
  2. 2
    24 hours before: Avoid complex carbohydrates — no beans, lentils, whole grains, or starchy vegetables.
  3. 3
    12 hours before: Fast completely — water only. No smoking. Avoid vigorous exercise.
  4. 4
    Morning of test: Do not brush teeth with toothpaste or use mouthwash. No probiotics. Water is fine.
  5. 5
    Baseline reading: Take a resting breath sample before consuming anything.
  6. 6
    Consume substrate: Drink the lactulose or glucose solution provided, mixed in 8 oz water within 5 minutes.
  7. 7
    Readings every 20 minutes for 3 hours. Remain seated between readings — no eating, drinking, or smoking.
  8. 8
    Upload results via FoodMarble app and share your report access code with our office.
Positive threshold: Rise of ≥20 ppm hydrogen above baseline, or ≥10 ppm methane above baseline, within the first 90 minutes.
  1. 1
    24 hours before: Avoid all dairy and fermented foods.
  2. 2
    12 hours before: Fast completely — water only.
  3. 3
    Substrate: Consume 25g lactose dissolved in 8 oz water within 5 minutes.
  4. 4
    Readings every 30 minutes for 3 hours. Record all symptoms (bloating, cramping, diarrhea).
  1. 1
    24 hours before: Avoid high-fructose fruits (apples, pears, mangoes, watermelon), honey, agave, HFCS.
  2. 2
    12 hours before: Fast completely — water only.
  3. 3
    Substrate: Consume 25g fructose dissolved in 8 oz water within 5 minutes.
  4. 4
    Readings every 30 minutes for 3 hours. Record all symptoms.

Hydrogen (H₂) — normal

Baseline under 20 ppm. Rise less than 20 ppm throughout. Bacteria are not fermenting the substrate in the small intestine.

Hydrogen — positive SIBO

Rise of ≥20 ppm above baseline within the first 90 minutes of the lactulose or glucose test.

Methane (CH₄) — IMO

Any reading ≥10 ppm above baseline suggests intestinal methanogen overgrowth, associated with constipation-predominant symptoms.

Flat-line result

If neither rises meaningfully, SIBO is unlikely. A small number of patients produce hydrogen sulfide — not measurable by standard devices — and may need further evaluation.

Results are interpreted by your physician in the context of your full clinical picture. Do not self-treat based on these results.
Understanding Your Pathology Report
Hyperplastic polyp
+
Very common and considered benign. No meaningful cancer risk. Does not typically shorten your surveillance interval.
Tubular adenoma (1–2, small)
+
Pre-cancerous but low risk when found early and completely removed. Typically leads to a 3–5 year follow-up colonoscopy recommendation.
Tubulovillous or villous adenoma
+
Higher cancer potential than tubular adenomas. Requires closer follow-up — typically 1–3 years depending on size and completeness of removal.
Low-grade vs. high-grade dysplasia
+
Dysplasia describes how abnormal cells look under a microscope. Low-grade is expected in any adenoma. High-grade carries higher risk — your physician will discuss a specific follow-up plan directly with you.
Sessile serrated lesion (SSL / SSA)
+
A distinct polyp type with its own cancer risk pathway. Guidelines generally recommend a 3-year follow-up colonoscopy, depending on size and whether dysplasia is present.
Chronic inactive gastritis / H. pylori
+
If H. pylori bacteria is identified, antibiotics plus acid suppression therapy are prescribed. A follow-up test (breath or stool antigen) is done 4–6 weeks after treatment to confirm eradication.
"Fragments of colonic mucosa, no dysplasia" — is this normal?
+
Yes — completely normal. Colon tissue was sampled and no abnormal changes were found. No further action needed.
Eosinophilic esophagitis (EoE) on biopsy
+
A chronic immune-mediated condition where eosinophils accumulate in the esophagus. Treatment may include dietary elimination, topical steroids, PPIs, or biologic therapy (dupilumab).
Your physician will contact you directly for any result requiring follow-up. Do not adjust care plans based solely on this page.

Billing & Insurance FAQ
Why did I receive two or three separate bills?
+
Standard for GI procedures. You may receive separate bills from: (1) our physician group, (2) the facility (surgery center or hospital), and (3) a pathology lab if biopsies were taken. Each is processed independently by your insurance.
My colonoscopy was "screening" — why was I billed?
+
If a polyp was removed, some insurance plans reclassify the visit as "diagnostic" — the "polyp rule." This can result in a deductible or co-pay. Contact your insurance carrier to understand your specific plan benefits.
What is the pathology charge?
+
If biopsies or polyps were sent to a lab, you will receive a separate bill from that laboratory — independent of physician and facility charges.
What is the anesthesia / CRNA charge?
+
If monitored anesthesia was used, an anesthesiologist or CRNA administered your sedation and bills separately. Confirm anesthesia coverage with your carrier before your procedure when possible.
How do I set up a payment plan?
+
Our billing department offers payment plans and financial hardship assistance. Call the number on your statement to discuss options.
What are my rights under the No Surprises Act?
+
Federal law (effective 2022) protects patients from unexpected out-of-network bills in many situations. If you received a surprise balance bill, contact your insurance or visit cms.gov/nosurprises.
For account-specific billing questions, please call the number on your statement.

Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS)

A functional gut-brain disorder causing chronic abdominal pain, bloating, and altered bowel habits. Not inflammatory — does not cause tissue damage or increase cancer risk. Diagnosis is based on Rome IV criteria.

Subtypes

IBS-D (diarrhea predominant), IBS-C (constipation predominant), IBS-M (mixed), IBS-U (unclassified).

Common triggers

High-FODMAP foods, stress, hormonal changes, antibiotics, prior GI infection (post-infectious IBS).

Management

Low-FODMAP diet, gut-directed CBT or hypnotherapy, antispasmodics, rifaximin (IBS-D), linaclotide or plecanatide (IBS-C).

Alarm features — report to your doctor

Rectal bleeding, unintentional weight loss, nighttime symptoms, fever, or family history of colon cancer or IBD.

Chronic Constipation

Chronic constipation is defined as fewer than 3 spontaneous bowel movements per week, often accompanied by straining, hard stools, a sense of incomplete evacuation, or bloating. It may be primary (functional) or secondary to medications, metabolic conditions, or structural problems.

Common causes

Low fiber/fluid intake, sedentary lifestyle, medications (opioids, calcium channel blockers, iron), hypothyroidism, diabetes, pelvic floor dysfunction, slow-transit constipation.

Evaluation

History and physical exam, colonoscopy if alarm features are present, anorectal manometry or defecography for suspected pelvic floor dysfunction, colonic transit study if slow transit is suspected.

Treatment

Increased fiber and fluid intake, exercise, osmotic laxatives (MiraLAX, lactulose), stimulant laxatives, secretagogues (linaclotide, plecanatide, lubiprostone), prucalopride for severe slow-transit constipation.

Alarm features

Rectal bleeding, weight loss, new constipation after age 45, family history of colorectal cancer — warrant colonoscopy evaluation.

Gastroparesis

Gastroparesis is delayed gastric emptying in the absence of mechanical obstruction. The stomach muscles do not contract normally, causing food to remain in the stomach too long. The most common causes are diabetes, post-surgical, and idiopathic (unknown cause).

Symptoms

Nausea, vomiting (often of undigested food), early satiety, postprandial fullness, bloating, abdominal pain, unintentional weight loss.

Diagnosis

Gastric emptying scintigraphy (nuclear medicine scan) is the gold standard. Upper endoscopy is performed first to rule out obstruction. Wireless motility capsule is an alternative test.

Dietary management

Small, frequent meals (4–6 per day). Low-fat, low-fiber foods. Soft or liquid textures. Avoid carbonated beverages, alcohol, and large meals. A registered dietitian consultation is recommended.

Medical treatment

Prokinetic agents (metoclopramide, domperidone, erythromycin). Antiemetics for symptom control. Glycemic control is critical in diabetic gastroparesis. Gastric electrical stimulation for refractory cases.

Fecal Incontinence

Fecal incontinence is the involuntary loss of bowel control, leading to unexpected leakage of stool. It is more common than often reported, as many patients are reluctant to discuss it. It significantly impacts quality of life but is treatable.

Common causes

Anal sphincter injury (obstetric trauma, prior surgery), rectal prolapse, chronic diarrhea, neurological conditions (diabetes, multiple sclerosis), rectal urgency from IBD or IBS.

Evaluation

Anorectal manometry measures sphincter pressure and rectal sensation. Endoanal ultrasound evaluates sphincter anatomy. Defecography assesses rectal prolapse or structural abnormalities.

Treatment

Dietary modification, bulking agents, antidiarrheals (loperamide), biofeedback and pelvic floor physical therapy, sacral nerve stimulation (SNS), sphincter repair surgery for selected patients.

Important note

Fecal incontinence is a medical condition, not a normal part of aging. Please discuss symptoms openly with your physician — effective treatments are available.

Pelvic Floor Dysfunction

Pelvic floor dysfunction (dyssynergic defecation) occurs when the pelvic floor muscles and external anal sphincter fail to coordinate properly during defecation. Instead of relaxing to allow stool passage, they paradoxically contract, causing obstructed defecation.

Symptoms

Excessive straining, inability to defecate despite urge, sensation of incomplete evacuation, need for manual maneuvers to pass stool, chronic constipation unresponsive to laxatives.

Diagnosis

Anorectal manometry with balloon expulsion test is the primary diagnostic tool. Defecography (X-ray or MRI) evaluates structural and functional abnormalities during defecation.

Treatment

Biofeedback therapy is the most effective treatment — retraining the pelvic floor muscles through guided exercises and sensory feedback. Pelvic floor physical therapy. Botulinum toxin injection for refractory cases.

Related conditions

Pelvic floor dysfunction may coexist with IBS-C, chronic pelvic pain, interstitial cystitis, and in women, pelvic organ prolapse.

Sphincter of Oddi Dysfunction (SOD)

The sphincter of Oddi is a muscular valve that controls the flow of bile and pancreatic juice into the small intestine. Sphincter of Oddi dysfunction occurs when this valve does not open properly, causing pain and potentially elevated liver or pancreatic enzymes. It is most commonly encountered in patients who have had their gallbladder removed.

Symptoms

Episodic right upper quadrant or epigastric pain, often severe and lasting 30+ minutes. May be accompanied by nausea and vomiting. Symptoms may mimic biliary colic after cholecystectomy.

Types

Biliary SOD (affecting bile flow) and pancreatic SOD (affecting pancreatic drainage). Classification is based on symptom pattern, liver enzyme elevation, and bile duct dilation.

Diagnosis

Liver function tests and bilirubin during pain episodes. MRCP or EUS to evaluate bile and pancreatic ducts. Sphincter of Oddi manometry via ERCP in selected cases.

Treatment

Smooth muscle relaxants, calcium channel blockers, endoscopic sphincterotomy (cutting the sphincter via ERCP) for selected patients with documented dysfunction. Management is individualized.

GERD (Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease)

GERD occurs when stomach acid regularly refluxes into the esophagus, causing symptoms or mucosal damage. It is one of the most common GI conditions in the United States. Chronic untreated GERD can progress to esophagitis, Barrett's esophagus, and in rare cases esophageal adenocarcinoma.

Common symptoms

Heartburn, regurgitation, chronic cough, hoarseness, lump-in-throat sensation (globus), difficulty swallowing, chest discomfort.

Lifestyle changes

Elevate head of bed 6–8 inches, avoid eating within 3 hours of bedtime, lose weight if overweight, avoid caffeine, alcohol, chocolate, fatty and spicy foods, peppermint.

Medical treatment

PPIs (omeprazole, pantoprazole, esomeprazole) — take 30–60 minutes before first meal. H2 blockers for milder symptoms. Long-term PPI use requires periodic reassessment.

When endoscopy is needed

Alarm symptoms (dysphagia, weight loss, bleeding), symptoms unresponsive to treatment, chronic GERD to screen for Barrett's esophagus, or if diagnosis is uncertain.

Barrett's Esophagus

Barrett's esophagus is a complication of chronic GERD in which the normal squamous lining of the lower esophagus is replaced by intestinal-type (columnar) epithelium. This change, called intestinal metaplasia, carries an increased risk of progression to esophageal adenocarcinoma. However, the absolute annual risk of cancer in Barrett's is low (approximately 0.1–0.3% per year).

Who is at risk?

Chronic GERD (over 5 years), male sex, age over 50, obesity (especially central), smoking history, white race, family history of Barrett's or esophageal adenocarcinoma.

Diagnosis

Upper endoscopy with biopsies. Barrett's is diagnosed when intestinal metaplasia is confirmed on pathology. Length of involvement (short segment vs. long segment) is documented.

Surveillance

Endoscopic surveillance intervals depend on the degree of dysplasia: no dysplasia (every 3–5 years), low-grade dysplasia (every 6–12 months or ablation), high-grade dysplasia (ablation recommended).

Treatment

Aggressive acid suppression with PPI therapy. Endoscopic eradication therapy (radiofrequency ablation, endoscopic mucosal resection) for dysplastic Barrett's. Surgery reserved for advanced cases.

Eosinophilic Esophagitis (EoE)

A chronic immune-mediated condition where eosinophils accumulate in the esophageal lining, causing inflammation, tissue remodeling, and narrowing. It is increasingly recognized in adults and children.

Symptoms

Difficulty swallowing (dysphagia), food getting stuck (food impaction), heartburn unresponsive to PPIs, chest pain, vomiting in children.

Common food triggers

Milk, wheat, eggs, soy, tree nuts, seafood — the "big six" elimination targets.

Treatment

Topical corticosteroids (budesonide, fluticasone), PPIs, dietary elimination (2-food, 4-food, or 6-food), dupilumab (biologic) for moderate-severe disease.

Monitoring

Repeat endoscopy with biopsies to assess treatment response. Esophageal dilation for strictures or rings causing significant dysphagia.

Achalasia

Achalasia is a rare motility disorder of the esophagus in which the lower esophageal sphincter (LES) fails to relax properly and the esophagus loses its normal peristaltic contractions. Food and liquid accumulate in the esophagus and cannot pass normally into the stomach.

Symptoms

Progressive difficulty swallowing both solids and liquids, regurgitation of undigested food (especially at night), chest pain or pressure, weight loss, recurrent aspiration or pneumonia.

Diagnosis

High-resolution esophageal manometry is the gold standard. Barium swallow shows a "bird's beak" narrowing at the LES. Upper endoscopy rules out pseudoachalasia from malignancy.

Treatment

Pneumatic dilation (endoscopic balloon stretching of the LES), Heller myotomy (surgical cutting of the LES muscle), or POEM (Per-Oral Endoscopic Myotomy — a minimally invasive endoscopic procedure). Botulinum toxin injection for patients who are poor candidates for other procedures.

Types

Type I (classic), Type II (most common, best treatment response), and Type III (spastic achalasia). Classified by high-resolution manometry patterns.

Esophageal Spasm

Esophageal spasm refers to abnormal, uncoordinated contractions of the esophageal muscle. The two main types are distal esophageal spasm (DES) and jackhammer esophagus (hypercontractile esophagus). These are motility disorders diagnosed by high-resolution manometry.

Symptoms

Intermittent chest pain (can mimic cardiac pain), difficulty swallowing, regurgitation. Symptoms are often unpredictable and can be triggered by hot or cold liquids, stress, or eating quickly.

Diagnosis

High-resolution esophageal manometry is the definitive test. Upper endoscopy and cardiac evaluation are performed first to rule out structural and cardiac causes of chest pain.

Treatment

Smooth muscle relaxants (nitrates, calcium channel blockers), tricyclic antidepressants or SSRIs for pain modulation, peppermint oil, endoscopic botulinum toxin injection, or POEM for refractory cases.

Reassurance

Esophageal spasm is not dangerous and does not increase cancer risk. Many patients improve with reassurance, dietary modifications, and stress management.

Esophageal Stricture

An esophageal stricture is a narrowing of the esophagus that causes difficulty swallowing. Strictures can be benign (non-cancerous) or malignant. The most common causes of benign strictures are chronic GERD (Schatzki ring, peptic stricture), eosinophilic esophagitis, and prior radiation therapy.

Symptoms

Progressive difficulty swallowing solids more than liquids, food impaction, regurgitation, weight loss, sensation of food sticking in the chest or throat.

Diagnosis

Upper endoscopy is both diagnostic and therapeutic. Barium swallow identifies the level and degree of narrowing. Biopsies are taken to rule out malignancy or EoE.

Treatment

Endoscopic dilation (bougie or balloon dilation) is the primary treatment for benign strictures. Multiple sessions may be required for fibrotic strictures. Acid suppression treats the underlying GERD. Steroid injection for refractory strictures.

Malignant strictures

Strictures caused by esophageal cancer require oncologic evaluation. Endoscopic stent placement may be used for palliation of malignant dysphagia.

Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD)

IBD encompasses Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis — chronic, relapsing-remitting immune-mediated conditions causing GI inflammation and tissue damage. Unlike IBS, IBD causes measurable structural damage and requires ongoing medical management.

Crohn's disease

Can involve any GI segment from mouth to anus. Full-thickness (transmural) inflammation, skip lesions, risk of fistulas, abscesses, and strictures.

Ulcerative colitis

Involves the colon and rectum only. Continuous mucosal inflammation. Long-standing pancolitis increases colorectal cancer risk and requires surveillance colonoscopy.

Treatment

Aminosalicylates (UC), immunomodulators (azathioprine, methotrexate), biologics (anti-TNF, anti-integrin, IL-12/23 inhibitors), JAK inhibitors, corticosteroids for flares.

Monitoring

Regular labs (CBC, CRP, drug levels), fecal calprotectin, periodic endoscopic surveillance, bone density assessment for steroid-exposed patients.

Celiac Disease

An autoimmune condition triggered by gluten ingestion that damages the small intestinal villi, causing malabsorption. Affects approximately 1% of the population — many cases go undiagnosed or present atypically.

Symptoms

Diarrhea, fatigue, weight loss, iron-deficiency anemia, bone loss, elevated liver enzymes, skin rash (dermatitis herpetiformis), infertility, neurological symptoms.

Diagnosis

Serology (tTG-IgA, total IgA) followed by upper endoscopy with duodenal biopsies. Must be tested while on a gluten-containing diet — a gluten-free diet before testing invalidates results.

Treatment

Strict lifelong gluten-free diet — the only proven treatment. Dietitian counseling strongly recommended. See the Diet section of this site for gluten-free guidance.

Follow-up

Repeat serology at 6–12 months. Check for nutritional deficiencies (iron, B12, folate, vitamin D, zinc). DEXA scan for bone density if indicated.

Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori)

H. pylori is a spiral-shaped bacterium that colonizes the stomach lining. It is one of the most common chronic bacterial infections worldwide, affecting approximately 50% of the global population. H. pylori is the leading cause of peptic ulcer disease and is classified as a Group I carcinogen for gastric cancer.

Symptoms

Many people are asymptomatic. When symptoms occur: burning or gnawing stomach pain (often relieved by eating or antacids), nausea, loss of appetite, frequent belching, bloating. Ulcers may cause more severe pain, vomiting, or GI bleeding.

Testing methods

Urea breath test (non-invasive, best for confirming eradication), stool antigen test, upper endoscopy with gastric biopsies (most accurate, also allows ulcer evaluation), serology (blood test — cannot confirm active infection).

Treatment

Antibiotic-based eradication regimens (typically 10–14 days): standard triple therapy, bismuth quadruple therapy, or vonoprazan-based regimens. Acid suppression with PPI is included in all regimens. Antibiotic resistance guides regimen selection.

Confirming eradication

A urea breath test or stool antigen test is performed at least 4 weeks after completing treatment to confirm H. pylori has been eradicated. Testing must be done off PPIs for at least 2 weeks before the breath test.

SIBO / IMO (Small Intestinal Bacterial & Methanogen Overgrowth)

SIBO occurs when bacteria that normally reside in the colon migrate into or proliferate excessively in the small intestine, causing fermentation, gas production, and symptoms. The methane-producing variant is now termed IMO (intestinal methanogen overgrowth).

Symptoms

Bloating (often worst after meals), belching, abdominal distension, diarrhea (SIBO) or constipation (IMO), nausea, and in severe cases weight loss and nutritional deficiencies.

Diagnosis

Lactulose or glucose hydrogen/methane breath test. See the Breath Testing section for our FoodMarble AIRE protocol and interpretation guide.

Treatment

Rifaximin (hydrogen SIBO), rifaximin + neomycin (IMO), elemental diet, prokinetics to address underlying motility issues, dietary modification.

Recurrence

SIBO frequently recurs without addressing predisposing factors (motility disorders, anatomic changes, acid suppression, prior surgery). Long-term management strategy is individualized.

Bile Acid Diarrhea (BAD) / Bile Acid Malabsorption

Bile acid diarrhea occurs when bile acids are not properly reabsorbed in the terminal ileum and spill into the colon, where they stimulate excess fluid secretion and rapid transit, causing chronic watery diarrhea. It is significantly underdiagnosed and is frequently mistaken for IBS-D.

Common causes

Cholecystectomy (gallbladder removal), Crohn's disease or resection of the terminal ileum, idiopathic (primary) bile acid malabsorption, microscopic colitis, radiation enteritis.

Symptoms

Chronic watery or urgency diarrhea, often worse after fatty meals, frequent bowel movements, fecal urgency, nocturnal diarrhea in some cases.

Diagnosis

SeHCAT scan (not available in the US), serum C4 (7α-hydroxy-4-cholesten-3-one) or FGF-19 levels, empirical trial of bile acid sequestrants, or SeHCAT where available.

Treatment

Bile acid sequestrants: cholestyramine, colesevelam (Welchol), or colestipol. Low-fat diet reduces symptoms. Response to treatment also confirms the diagnosis.

Exocrine Pancreatic Insufficiency (EPI)

Exocrine pancreatic insufficiency occurs when the pancreas does not produce enough digestive enzymes to properly break down food, leading to malabsorption of fat, protein, and fat-soluble vitamins. EPI is most commonly caused by chronic pancreatitis, pancreatic cancer, or cystic fibrosis.

Symptoms

Steatorrhea (oily, foul-smelling, floating stools), chronic diarrhea, significant unintentional weight loss, bloating, flatulence, nutritional deficiencies (vitamins A, D, E, K, B12).

Causes

Chronic pancreatitis (most common), pancreatic cancer or surgery, cystic fibrosis, Shwachman-Diamond syndrome, celiac disease (secondary EPI), and diabetes (type 3c).

Diagnosis

Fecal elastase-1 test (stool test — low levels indicate EPI). Fecal fat quantification (72-hour collection). Imaging (CT, MRI) to evaluate pancreatic structure. Serum nutritional panel.

Treatment

Pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT) — taken with every meal and snack. Fat-soluble vitamin supplementation. Low-fat diet helps reduce symptoms. Treat underlying cause when possible.

Cirrhosis

Cirrhosis is the end-stage of chronic liver disease characterized by extensive scarring (fibrosis) that disrupts normal liver architecture and function. The most common causes in the United States are chronic hepatitis C, alcoholic liver disease, and nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH/MASLD).

Compensated vs. decompensated

Compensated cirrhosis may be asymptomatic for years. Decompensated cirrhosis involves complications: ascites (fluid in abdomen), hepatic encephalopathy, variceal bleeding, spontaneous bacterial peritonitis, or hepatorenal syndrome.

Diagnosis

Liver function tests, platelet count, imaging (ultrasound, CT, MRI), FibroScan (liver stiffness measurement), and in some cases liver biopsy for definitive staging.

Complications & monitoring

Upper endoscopy to screen for esophageal varices. Liver ultrasound every 6 months with AFP for hepatocellular carcinoma surveillance. DEXA scan for osteoporosis. Management of portal hypertension.

Treatment

Treat underlying cause (antiviral therapy for hepatitis, alcohol cessation, weight loss for MASLD). Manage complications (diuretics for ascites, beta-blockers for varices, lactulose/rifaximin for encephalopathy). Liver transplant evaluation for advanced disease.

Condition descriptions are educational overviews. Your physician will tailor diagnosis and treatment to your specific situation. Contact our office with any questions.
GI Cancers — Overview & Screening

Early detection is the most important factor in GI cancer outcomes. This section provides general educational information about the most common GI cancers. If you have concerns about symptoms or family history, please discuss them with your physician.

Colorectal Cancer (CRC)

Colorectal cancer is the third most common cancer and second leading cause of cancer death in the United States. The vast majority arise from adenomatous polyps over 10–15 years — making colonoscopy one of the most powerful cancer prevention tools available. Most cases are preventable through screening.

Screening — when to start

Average risk: begin at age 45. High risk (family history of CRC or advanced polyps in a first-degree relative before age 60): begin at age 40 or 10 years before the youngest affected relative's diagnosis, whichever is earlier. IBD patients: surveillance colonoscopy per gastroenterologist's schedule.

Screening options

Colonoscopy every 10 years (gold standard — can detect and remove polyps in one procedure). Cologuard (stool DNA test) every 3 years. FIT (fecal immunochemical test) annually. Positive non-colonoscopy tests require follow-up colonoscopy.

Symptoms

Change in bowel habits lasting more than 4 weeks, rectal bleeding or blood in stool, persistent abdominal discomfort, a feeling that the bowel does not empty completely, unexplained weight loss, fatigue from iron-deficiency anemia.

Risk factors

Age over 45, personal or family history of polyps or CRC, IBD, Lynch syndrome or FAP, obesity, physical inactivity, heavy alcohol use, smoking, red and processed meat consumption.

Pancreatic Cancer

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is the most common type of pancreatic cancer and one of the most lethal malignancies, largely because it is often asymptomatic until advanced stages. Early detection — when surgical resection is still possible — dramatically improves outcomes.

Symptoms

Painless jaundice (yellowing of skin/eyes), new-onset diabetes especially over age 50, unexplained weight loss, mid-back or upper abdominal pain, loss of appetite, light-colored stools and dark urine, acute pancreatitis without clear cause.

Risk factors

Smoking, obesity, chronic pancreatitis, new-onset diabetes (type 3c), family history (BRCA1/2, Lynch syndrome, PALB2, ATM mutations), IPMN (intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm) cysts.

Diagnosis

CT scan with pancreatic protocol, MRI/MRCP, endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) with FNA/FNB biopsy. CA 19-9 tumor marker (not specific enough for screening but useful for monitoring).

High-risk surveillance

Patients with strong family history or genetic mutations (BRCA2, PALB2, ATM, Lynch syndrome) with a first-degree relative with pancreatic cancer may qualify for surveillance with EUS or MRI — discuss with your physician.

Esophageal Cancer

There are two main types of esophageal cancer: esophageal adenocarcinoma (arising from Barrett's esophagus, related to GERD) and squamous cell carcinoma (related to smoking and alcohol, arising in the upper/middle esophagus). Both present similarly but have different risk profiles.

Symptoms

Progressive dysphagia (difficulty swallowing) — first solids, then liquids, unexplained weight loss, chest pain or pressure, hoarseness, chronic cough, vomiting, or regurgitation.

Risk factors — adenocarcinoma

Chronic GERD, Barrett's esophagus, obesity, male sex, age over 50, smoking, white race.

Risk factors — squamous cell

Heavy alcohol use, tobacco smoking, achalasia, caustic ingestion injury, poor nutritional status, HPV in some populations.

Prevention & surveillance

Aggressive GERD treatment and Barrett's surveillance are the primary prevention strategies for adenocarcinoma. Smoking cessation and alcohol reduction for squamous cell carcinoma risk reduction.

Gastric (Stomach) Cancer

Gastric cancer (stomach cancer) is the fifth most common cancer globally. The incidence has declined in Western countries due to refrigeration (reduced salt-preserved food consumption) and H. pylori eradication. However, it remains an important cancer to recognize early, as prognosis is significantly better when caught at an early stage.

Symptoms

Indigestion or heartburn that is new or worsening, persistent nausea, loss of appetite, unintentional weight loss, early satiety, vomiting, black tarry stools (GI bleeding), abdominal pain.

Risk factors

H. pylori infection (most important modifiable risk factor), smoking, family history, diet high in salted/smoked/preserved foods, atrophic gastritis, intestinal metaplasia, prior gastric surgery, East Asian ancestry.

Diagnosis

Upper endoscopy with biopsies is the primary diagnostic tool. CT scan for staging. EUS for local tumor staging. PET scan for metastatic evaluation.

H. pylori & prevention

H. pylori eradication reduces the risk of gastric cancer. Patients with intestinal metaplasia or dysplasia on gastric biopsies require endoscopic surveillance per guidelines.

Biliary Tract Cancer (Cholangiocarcinoma)

Biliary tract cancers include cholangiocarcinoma (cancer of the bile ducts) and gallbladder cancer. They are relatively rare but often diagnosed at an advanced stage due to vague early symptoms. Intrahepatic, perihilar, and distal cholangiocarcinomas are classified by anatomic location.

Symptoms

Painless jaundice (yellowing of skin and eyes), dark urine, pale stools, right upper quadrant pain or discomfort, unexplained weight loss, itching (pruritus), fever from biliary obstruction.

Risk factors

Primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC), hepatitis B or C, cirrhosis, biliary cysts (choledochal cysts), liver fluke infection, inflammatory bowel disease (especially UC with PSC), obesity, diabetes.

Diagnosis

MRCP (MRI of bile ducts), CT scan, ERCP with brush cytology or biopsies, EUS-guided FNA/FNB, CA 19-9 and CEA tumor markers. Histologic confirmation is required for treatment planning.

Surveillance in PSC

Patients with primary sclerosing cholangitis have a significantly elevated lifetime risk of cholangiocarcinoma. Annual MRCP and CA 19-9 testing is recommended for PSC surveillance.

Hepatocellular Carcinoma (HCC)

Hepatocellular carcinoma is the most common primary liver cancer and typically arises in the setting of underlying chronic liver disease or cirrhosis. It is the sixth most common cancer worldwide. Regular surveillance in high-risk patients is critical because HCC detected at an early stage can often be cured.

Risk factors

Cirrhosis from any cause (hepatitis B, hepatitis C, alcohol, MASLD/NASH), chronic hepatitis B infection even without cirrhosis, aflatoxin exposure, hemochromatosis, alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, Wilson's disease.

Symptoms

HCC is often silent in early stages. When symptomatic: right upper quadrant pain or fullness, unexplained weight loss, loss of appetite, new or worsening jaundice, abdominal swelling (ascites), sudden deterioration in a cirrhotic patient.

Surveillance — who qualifies

All patients with cirrhosis, patients with chronic hepatitis B meeting specific criteria (age/family history/viral load), and certain patients with advanced fibrosis. Surveillance: liver ultrasound with AFP every 6 months.

Treatment

Depends on tumor size, number, liver function, and underlying disease: surgical resection, liver transplantation (curative for selected patients), ablation (RFA, microwave), TACE (transarterial chemoembolization), TARE (Y-90 radioembolization), or systemic therapy (atezolizumab + bevacizumab) for advanced HCC.

Important: This section provides general cancer education only. It is not a substitute for individualized screening recommendations from your physician. If you have symptoms, a significant family history, or known risk factors for any GI cancer, please schedule an appointment to discuss personalized screening and surveillance options.

Diet & Nutrition Guidance

The low-FODMAP diet reduces fermentable carbohydrates that trigger IBS symptoms. It is a temporary elimination protocol — not a permanent diet — followed by structured reintroduction.

Phase 1: Elimination (2–6 weeks)

Remove all high-FODMAP foods: garlic, onion, wheat, rye, lactose, apples, pears, legumes, cashews, honey, HFCS.

Phase 2: Reintroduction

Reintroduce one FODMAP group at a time over 6–8 weeks to identify personal triggers. Do not skip this phase.

Phase 3: Personalization

Maintain a diet based on individual tolerances — not a blanket low-FODMAP diet indefinitely.

Best resource

The Monash University FODMAP app is the gold-standard for current FODMAP ratings of specific foods and serving sizes.

✅ Foods to eat

White bread, white rice, pasta, eggs, tender chicken or fish, well-cooked vegetables without skins, canned fruit without seeds, dairy in small amounts.

❌ Foods to avoid

Whole grains, nuts, seeds, raw vegetables, corn, popcorn, dried fruit, berries, beans, lentils, tough meats, foods with skins or hulls.

Days 1–3: Soft diet

Eggs, yogurt, bananas, applesauce, white toast, rice, soft pasta, soup.

Avoid for 7 days

Seeds, nuts, popcorn, raw vegetables, spicy foods, NSAIDs, aspirin. Tylenol is safe for pain.

Please discuss blood thinner use with your physician prior to discontinuation.

Grains to avoid

Wheat (all forms: spelt, kamut, farro, durum, semolina), barley, rye, triticale. Oats only if certified gluten-free.

Hidden gluten

Soy sauce, malt vinegar, beer, many condiments, modified food starch, some medications and supplements.

Safe grains

Rice, corn, potatoes, quinoa, buckwheat, millet, certified GF oats, teff, amaranth, sorghum.

Cross-contamination

Use separate cutting boards and toasters. When dining out, specify celiac disease — dedicated fryers and prep surfaces matter.

A registered dietitian with GI specialty can provide individualized guidance — ask our office for a referral.
Medications Guide
Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) — omeprazole, pantoprazole, esomeprazole
+
PPIs reduce acid production in the stomach. Used for GERD, ulcers, H. pylori treatment, and EoE. Take on an empty stomach 30–60 minutes before your first meal. Long-term use requires periodic reassessment.
Rifaximin (Xifaxan)
+
A non-absorbed, gut-selective antibiotic for SIBO and IBS-D. Stays in the GI tract with minimal systemic absorption. Typically taken three times daily for 14 days, with or without food.
Mesalamine (Lialda, Asacol HD, Pentasa)
+
Anti-inflammatory medication for mild-to-moderate ulcerative colitis. Works locally in the colon. Available as oral tablets, suppositories, and enemas. Take as directed — formulations vary in dosing schedule.
Budesonide (Entocort EC, Uceris)
+
Locally-acting corticosteroid with less systemic absorption than prednisone. Entocort EC for Crohn's involving the ileum/right colon. Uceris for ulcerative colitis. Used short-term for induction of remission.
Biologics (infliximab, adalimumab, vedolizumab, ustekinumab)
+
Targeted therapies that block specific immune pathways in IBD and EoE. Given by injection or IV infusion on a schedule. TB testing, hepatitis B screening, and vaccine updates required before starting. Report any signs of infection promptly.
Linaclotide (Linzess) / Plecanatide (Trulance)
+
Used for IBS-C and chronic constipation. Work locally to increase intestinal fluid and accelerate transit. Linzess taken on an empty stomach 30 minutes before breakfast. Diarrhea is the most common side effect.
Never stop, start, or adjust a prescribed medication without consulting your physician.
Hemorrhoid Banding (Rubber Band Ligation)

Rubber band ligation is the most effective office-based treatment for internal hemorrhoids (grades I–III). A small elastic band is placed around the base of the hemorrhoid, cutting off its blood supply. The hemorrhoid shrinks and falls off within 7–10 days.

Who is it for?

Internal hemorrhoids causing bleeding, prolapse, or discomfort that has not responded to dietary and lifestyle changes.

Success rate

Approximately 70–80% effective per session. Most patients require 2–3 sessions spaced 4–6 weeks apart.

What it does NOT treat

External hemorrhoids (outside the anal opening) cannot be banded. Surgical hemorrhoidectomy may be needed for large external or mixed hemorrhoids.

Duration

The banding itself takes only a few minutes and is typically performed in the office during a colonoscopy or flexible sigmoidoscopy visit.

  1. 1
    If banding is performed at the time of a colonoscopy, follow your standard bowel prep instructions.
  2. 2
    If performed as a standalone office procedure, a Fleet enema the morning of the visit is typically sufficient. Follow the specific instructions given at scheduling.
  3. 3
    Blood thinners: Please discuss blood thinner use with your physician prior to discontinuation.
  4. 4
    Eat a light meal beforehand unless instructed otherwise. No sedation is typically required for in-office banding.
  1. 1
    Diet: Increase fiber intake (25–35g per day) and fluid intake. Soft, regular stools reduce straining and promote healing. A stool softener (docusate) may be recommended.
  2. 2
    Activity: Avoid heavy lifting, straining, and strenuous exercise for 48–72 hours.
  3. 3
    Pain management: Mild discomfort and pressure for 1–3 days is normal. Tylenol is appropriate. Avoid NSAIDs for at least 7 days.
  4. 4
    Band passage: The band and hemorrhoid tissue fall off within 7–10 days. A small amount of blood or tissue in the toilet at that time is normal.
  5. 5
    Sitz baths: Sitting in warm water for 10–15 minutes 2–3 times per day can relieve discomfort.
Call our office immediately or go to the ER if you experience: heavy rectal bleeding (soaking multiple pads), fever above 101°F, severe pain unrelieved by Tylenol, difficulty urinating, or signs of infection.

During the procedure

You will be positioned on your side. A small anoscope is inserted to visualize the hemorrhoid. The band placement takes seconds — you may feel pressure but typically not sharp pain.

Immediately after

A sensation of fullness, pressure, or mild aching is normal for 24–48 hours. Most patients return to normal activity the same day.

Days 2–10

The banded tissue gradually shrinks and falls off. Some spotting of blood is expected. Avoid constipation — straining increases bleeding risk.

Follow-up

A follow-up visit is scheduled 4–6 weeks later. If multiple hemorrhoids are present, additional banding sessions are performed at these visits.

Follow all specific instructions provided by your physician at the time of your procedure.
Capsule Endoscopy Prep Guide

Capsule endoscopy (the "pill camera") allows your doctor to examine the entire small intestine — a region standard endoscopy and colonoscopy cannot reach. You swallow a vitamin-sized capsule containing a tiny camera that takes thousands of photos as it passes naturally through your digestive tract.

Why it's ordered

Obscure GI bleeding, unexplained iron-deficiency anemia, Crohn's disease evaluation, small bowel tumors, celiac disease assessment.

How long?

You wear a recording device for approximately 8–10 hours. The capsule passes naturally in a bowel movement within 24–48 hours.

Is it safe?

Yes for most patients. If you have known bowel narrowing (stricture), swallowing difficulties, or a pacemaker, notify our office before the test.

Do I need sedation?

No — you simply swallow the capsule with water. Most patients go about their normal day during the recording period.

  1. 1
    3–5 days before: Stop iron supplements — iron can coat the bowel lining and interfere with image quality.
  2. 2
    Day before: Eat a low-fiber, low-residue diet. Avoid red or orange colored foods and drinks.
  3. 3
    Day before after 9:00 PM: Nothing to eat. Clear liquids only (water, white grape juice, plain broth).
  4. 4
    Bowel prep (if prescribed): Some physicians prescribe a prep to improve small bowel visibility. Follow your specific instructions if a prep was ordered.
  5. 5
    Medications: Take essential morning medications with a small sip of water at least 1 hour before swallowing the capsule. Hold GLP-1 medications (Ozempic, Wegovy) — contact our office for guidance.
If you have a cardiac pacemaker, ICD, or neurostimulator, notify our office immediately — special precautions or device compatibility checks are required.
  1. 1
    Arrive at the designated location. The sensor belt or vest will be fitted to your abdomen.
  2. 2
    Swallow the capsule with a small amount of water.
  3. 3
    First 2 hours: Nothing to eat or drink. Remain upright (sitting or walking).
  4. 4
    After 2 hours: You may drink clear liquids.
  5. 5
    After 4 hours: You may eat a light snack (crackers, toast).
  6. 6
    Avoid MRI scans and strong magnetic fields during the recording period. Avoid strenuous exercise and repeated bending.
  7. 7
    Return the recording device at the end of the recording period as instructed.
  1. 1
    Resume normal diet and activities once the recording device is returned.
  2. 2
    The capsule passes naturally within 24–72 hours. You do not need to retrieve it.
  3. 3
    Your physician will review the images (this takes several days) and contact you with results.
  4. 4
    If you do not pass the capsule within 2 weeks, contact our office. Capsule retention is rare but can occur with strictures.
Call our office or go to the ER if you experience: difficulty swallowing after taking the capsule, significant abdominal pain or distension, nausea, or vomiting.
Always follow the specific instructions provided by our office at scheduling.
FibroScan® — Liver Stiffness Testing

FibroScan® is a non-invasive, painless ultrasound-based test that measures liver stiffness (fibrosis) and fat content (steatosis). It is used as an alternative to liver biopsy to assess liver damage in NAFLD/MASLD, hepatitis B and C, alcoholic liver disease, and other chronic liver conditions.

Why it's ordered

To assess liver fibrosis stage, monitor disease progression, evaluate fatty liver disease (NAFLD/MASLD), or follow up after hepatitis treatment.

How it works

A probe placed on the skin over the liver sends a mild vibration and ultrasound wave. The speed of the wave indicates liver stiffness. Takes 5–10 minutes.

Is it painful?

No — completely painless. You will feel only a mild vibration on the right side of your abdomen. No needles, no sedation, no radiation.

Limitations

Results may be less reliable with very high BMI, active hepatitis inflammation, or significant ascites. Your physician will factor this into interpretation.

  1. 1
    Fast for at least 2–3 hours before the test. Eating causes temporary changes in liver blood flow that can affect results. Water is fine.
  2. 2
    Avoid alcohol for at least 24 hours before your appointment.
  3. 3
    Wear comfortable, loose-fitting clothing that allows easy access to the right side of your abdomen.
  4. 4
    Continue all regular medications unless instructed otherwise.
FibroScan requires no bowel prep, sedation, or IV access. You can drive yourself and return to normal activity immediately after.

FibroScan measures two values — liver stiffness (kPa, reflecting fibrosis) and CAP score (dB/m, reflecting fat content). Results are interpreted by your physician alongside your labs and clinical history.

Liver stiffness (kPa)

Lower values (under 7 kPa) generally suggest minimal fibrosis. Higher values suggest advancing fibrosis or cirrhosis. Your physician will discuss your specific result in context.

CAP score — fat content

Measures hepatic steatosis (liver fat). Higher scores indicate greater fat accumulation. Used to grade and monitor fatty liver disease.

What happens next?

Depending on results, your physician may recommend lifestyle changes, medication, additional imaging (MRI), or in rare cases, liver biopsy for definitive staging.

Can it replace biopsy?

In many cases, yes. FibroScan reduces the need for invasive biopsy. However, biopsy may still be needed when results are inconclusive.

FibroScan results are interpreted by your physician together with your labs, symptoms, and medical history. Do not interpret results in isolation.

Endoscopic Ultrasound (EUS)

Endoscopic ultrasound combines an endoscope with a high-frequency ultrasound probe to produce detailed images of the GI tract walls and nearby organs (pancreas, liver, bile ducts, lymph nodes). It can also obtain tissue samples (FNA/FNB) from deep structures.

Why it's ordered

Pancreatic cysts or masses, pancreatitis, bile duct stones, submucosal GI tumors, staging of GI cancers, unexplained abdominal pain.

Prep (upper EUS)

Same as upper endoscopy — nothing to eat or drink for 6–8 hours. IV sedation is used. Arrange a driver.

Prep (lower EUS)

If examining the rectum or lower GI tract, a bowel prep or enema may be required. Follow specific instructions provided.

If a biopsy is taken

Results return in 5–7 business days. Blood thinners may need to be held for 5–7 days after FNA/FNB.

EUS is a specialized procedure. Follow all instructions provided at scheduling.
ERCP — Bile Duct & Pancreas Procedure

ERCP (Endoscopic Retrograde Cholangiopancreatography) combines endoscopy and X-ray imaging to diagnose and treat problems in the bile ducts and pancreatic duct. It is used to remove bile duct stones, treat blockages, place stents, or evaluate unexplained jaundice.

Why it's ordered

Bile duct stones, strictures, pancreatic duct problems, jaundice, recurrent pancreatitis, or biliary leaks after surgery.

Prep

Nothing to eat or drink for 6–8 hours. IV sedation or general anesthesia is used. You will need a driver — do not drive for 24 hours.

Blood thinners

Please discuss blood thinner use with your physician prior to discontinuation.

After the procedure

Mild sore throat and bloating are common. Eat light foods several hours after. Watch for fever, severe abdominal pain, or jaundice — these require immediate attention.

Post-ERCP pancreatitis occurs in approximately 3–5% of cases. Symptoms include worsening abdominal pain radiating to the back, nausea, and vomiting within 24 hours. Go to the ER if these occur.
ERCP is a specialized procedure. All prep instructions will be provided at scheduling.
