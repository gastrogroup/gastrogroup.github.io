Bowel Prep Instructions

Evening before + morning of procedure — the most effective approach for colon cleansing.

  1. 1
    5 days before: Stop iron supplements and fiber supplements (Metamucil, Citrucel, Benefiber).
  2. 2
    3 days before: Begin a low-fiber diet. Avoid seeds, nuts, raw vegetables, salads, whole grains, and high-fiber fruits.
  3. 3
    Day before — noon onward: Clear liquids only. Water, plain broth, apple juice, white grape juice, black coffee or tea (no cream), plain gelatin. No red or purple colored liquids.
  4. 4
    Day before — 6:00 PM: Begin the first half of your prescribed prep solution. Drink one 8 oz glass every 15 minutes until half is finished.
  5. 5
    Day of procedure — 4 to 5 hours before arrival: Drink the second half of your prep solution at the same pace.
  6. 6
    2 hours before your scheduled arrival: Stop all liquids. Nothing by mouth from this point forward.
Medications: Continue blood pressure, heart, thyroid, and seizure medications with a small sip of water unless specifically instructed otherwise by our office. Hold blood thinners (warfarin, Xarelto, Eliquis, Plavix) only if instructed. Hold diabetes medications on the morning of the procedure.
Call our office if you are unable to complete the prep, have severe nausea or vomiting, experience chest pain, or are unsure whether to hold any medication.

Best suited for afternoon procedures or when split-dose is not practical.

  1. 1
    Evening before: Eat a low-fiber dinner by 7:00 PM, then switch to clear liquids only for the remainder of the evening.
  2. 2
    Morning of procedure (5–6 hours before your scheduled arrival): Begin the entire prep solution. Drink one 8 oz glass every 15 minutes until complete.
  3. 3
    2 hours before arrival: Nothing by mouth — no liquids, gum, or mints.
Your prep is considered adequate when your stool output appears clear yellow or pale — similar to apple juice or urine. If you are still passing brown or thick material, contact our office.

For patients who have difficulty tolerating large-volume preps, our physicians may prescribe one of the following lower-volume alternatives. Always follow the specific instructions on your prescription insert.

SUTAB (tablets)

12 tablets taken in two rounds with water. No large liquid volumes. Good option for patients who struggle with the taste of liquid preps.

Plenvu (1L PEG)

A low-volume PEG-based solution taken in two doses. Requires additional clear liquid hydration alongside.

MiraLAX + Gatorade

238g MiraLAX mixed into 64 oz Gatorade (non-red, non-purple). Often prescribed for patients with prior prep intolerance.

CLENPIQ (liquid)

Small-volume sodium picosulfate prep. Two small bottles taken 12 hours apart with required water intake.

If you have kidney disease, heart failure, or are over 75, contact our office before beginning any prep — some formulas require physician-specific selection.

Follow this diet for 3 days before your colonoscopy to improve prep quality.

✅ Foods you CAN eat

White bread, white rice, pasta, eggs, chicken, fish, well-cooked carrots or green beans (no skin), bananas, canned fruit (no seeds), yogurt, cheese, ice cream.

❌ Foods to AVOID

Whole grains, nuts, seeds, raw vegetables, salads, corn, popcorn, berries, apples with skin, beans, lentils, high-fiber cereals, and any supplement with fiber.

Prep instructions on this page are general guidelines. Always follow the specific written instructions provided to you at the time of scheduling, as your physician may have customized these based on your medical history.
Breath Testing — FoodMarble AIRE

Lactulose or glucose hydrogen/methane breath test for small intestinal bacterial overgrowth.

  1. 1
    48 hours before: Avoid high-fiber foods, fermented foods (yogurt, kefir, sauerkraut, kombucha), and probiotics.
  2. 2
    24 hours before: Avoid complex carbohydrates — no beans, lentils, whole grains, or starchy vegetables.
  3. 3
    12 hours before (night prior): Fast completely — nothing to eat or drink except plain water. No smoking. Avoid vigorous exercise.
  4. 4
    Morning of test: Do not brush teeth with toothpaste, use mouthwash, or take probiotics. Plain water is fine. Avoid sleeping with mouth open the night prior if possible.
  5. 5
    Baseline breath sample: Before consuming anything, take a resting baseline reading into the FoodMarble AIRE device.
  6. 6
    Consume substrate: Drink the lactulose or glucose solution provided by our office, mixed in 8 oz of water as directed. Consume within 5 minutes.
  7. 7
    Readings every 20 minutes for 3 hours (lactulose) or 2 hours (glucose). Remain seated and sedentary between readings. No eating, drinking, or smoking.
  8. 8
    Upload and share: Sync your FoodMarble app and share your test report access code with our office as instructed.
Positive threshold: A rise of ≥20 ppm hydrogen above baseline — or ≥10 ppm methane above baseline — within the first 90 minutes is considered positive for SIBO. Your physician will review all results and contact you.
  1. 1
    24 hours before: Avoid all dairy products, lactose-containing foods, and fermented foods.
  2. 2
    12 hours before: Complete fast — water only.
  3. 3
    Baseline reading: Take a breath sample before consuming the test substrate.
  4. 4
    Substrate: Consume 25g lactose dissolved in 8 oz water. Drink within 5 minutes.
  5. 5
    Readings every 30 minutes for 3 hours. Note and record any symptoms (bloating, cramping, diarrhea, gas).
Symptom tracking during the test is important — the combination of a positive breath result AND symptoms is the most clinically meaningful finding.
  1. 1
    24 hours before: Avoid high-fructose fruits (apples, pears, mangoes, watermelon), honey, agave, and high-fructose corn syrup.
  2. 2
    12 hours before: Complete fast — water only.
  3. 3
    Baseline reading: Take breath sample before substrate.
  4. 4
    Substrate: Consume 25g fructose dissolved in 8 oz water within 5 minutes.
  5. 5
    Readings every 30 minutes for 3 hours. Record symptoms throughout.

Hydrogen (H₂) — normal

Baseline under 20 ppm. Rise of less than 20 ppm above baseline throughout the test. Suggests bacteria are not fermenting the substrate in the small intestine.

Hydrogen — positive for SIBO

Rise of ≥20 ppm above baseline within the first 90 minutes of the lactulose or glucose test.

Methane (CH₄) — IMO

Any methane reading ≥10 ppm above baseline suggests intestinal methanogen overgrowth (IMO), associated with constipation-predominant symptoms.

Flat-line result

If neither hydrogen nor methane rises meaningfully, SIBO is unlikely. However, a small number of patients produce hydrogen sulfide — not measurable by standard devices — which may require further evaluation.

Results interpretation is performed by your physician in the context of your full clinical picture. Do not treat yourself based on these results alone.
Understanding Your Pathology Report

Pathology reports use specific medical terminology that can be confusing. Below are the most common findings our patients ask about, explained in plain language.

Hyperplastic polyp
+
Hyperplastic polyps are very common and are considered benign (non-cancerous). They have no meaningful cancer risk on their own. Finding hyperplastic polyps alone typically does not shorten your surveillance interval — your next colonoscopy will still be scheduled based on your other findings and personal risk factors.
Tubular adenoma (1–2, small)
+
Tubular adenomas are pre-cancerous polyps, but carry a low risk of progressing to cancer — especially when found early and completely removed. Finding one or two small tubular adenomas (under 10mm) typically results in a 3–5 year follow-up colonoscopy recommendation per current guidelines.
Tubulovillous or villous adenoma
+
These adenoma types have a higher-than-average potential for cancerous change compared to purely tubular adenomas. They require closer follow-up — typically a colonoscopy in 1–3 years depending on size, number, and completeness of removal.
Low-grade dysplasia vs. high-grade dysplasia
+
Dysplasia describes how abnormal the cells within a polyp look under a microscope. Low-grade dysplasia is the expected finding in any adenoma and is not an emergency. High-grade dysplasia means cells look very abnormal and carry a higher risk — your physician will discuss a specific follow-up plan with you directly and may recommend a sooner surveillance scope.
Sessile serrated lesion (SSL) / sessile serrated adenoma (SSA)
+
Sessile serrated lesions are a distinct polyp type that can be harder to detect and have their own cancer risk pathway. Guidelines generally recommend a 3-year follow-up colonoscopy when these are found, depending on size and whether there is associated dysplasia.
Chronic inactive gastritis / H. pylori
+
Chronic gastritis means there is inflammation of the stomach lining. If H. pylori bacteria is identified, a course of antibiotics plus acid suppression therapy is typically prescribed. A follow-up test (breath test or stool antigen test) is done 4–6 weeks after completing treatment to confirm eradication of the bacteria.
"Fragments of colonic mucosa, no dysplasia" — is this normal?
+
Yes. This is a completely normal result. It means colon tissue was sampled and no abnormal changes were found. No further action is needed based on this finding alone.
Eosinophilic esophagitis (EoE) on biopsy
+
Eosinophilic esophagitis is a chronic immune-mediated condition where white blood cells (eosinophils) accumulate in the esophageal lining. It often presents with difficulty swallowing or food impaction. Treatment may include dietary elimination, topical steroids, proton pump inhibitors, or newer biologic medications.
This guide is for general educational purposes. Your physician will contact you directly for any result requiring follow-up. Do not adjust medications or care plans based solely on this page.
After Your Procedure
  1. 1
    You will receive a written procedure summary before leaving the facility. This includes findings, any polyps removed, and your recommended surveillance interval.
  2. 2
    Biopsy results (if obtained) typically return within 3–7 business days. Our office will contact you with results by phone or patient portal message.
  3. 3
    Diet: Resume a regular diet as tolerated. Start light — soups, crackers, toast — before heavier meals. Bloating and gas are expected for 12–24 hours from the air used during the scope.
  4. 4
    Sedation: Do not drive, operate heavy machinery, drink alcohol, or make important legal or financial decisions for the remainder of the day. Have a responsible adult with you.
  5. 5
    If a polyp was removed: Avoid NSAIDs (ibuprofen, Advil, Aleve, naproxen) and aspirin for 7 days unless medically necessary. Acetaminophen (Tylenol) is safe.
  6. 6
    Activity: Avoid strenuous exercise and heavy lifting for 24 hours. Normal activity resumes the next day for most patients.
Go to the ER or call 911 if you experience: heavy rectal bleeding (more than a cup), severe or worsening abdominal pain, rigid abdomen, fever above 101°F, inability to pass gas or have a bowel movement after 3 days, or chest pain.
  1. 1
    Sore throat: A mild sore throat or throat irritation is common for 1–2 days. Ice chips, throat lozenges, and soft foods (yogurt, smoothies, soup) are soothing.
  2. 2
    Diet: Resume regular diet as tolerated unless a dilation was performed, in which case you will receive specific dietary instructions from our office.
  3. 3
    Biopsy results return in 3–7 business days. H. pylori culture results may take up to 2 weeks. We will contact you.
  4. 4
    Sedation: Same restrictions as colonoscopy — no driving, alcohol, or major decisions for the rest of the day.
Call our office or go to the ER if you experience: severe chest or throat pain, difficulty swallowing that worsens after the procedure, fever, or vomiting blood.
  1. 1
    Any labs, imaging, or prior records ordered at today's visit will be reviewed by your physician upon receipt. You do not need to call to confirm they were received.
  2. 2
    If a procedure was scheduled today, prep instructions will be mailed or sent via patient portal within 2 business days.
  3. 3
    New prescriptions sent electronically to your pharmacy may take 24–48 hours to process. Please contact your pharmacy first before calling our office about prescription status.
  4. 4
    Follow-up appointments can be scheduled by calling our office during business hours.
  5. 5
    If your physician ordered a breath test kit, you will receive instructions for home testing. This section of the website has a full FoodMarble protocol guide.
Billing & Insurance FAQ
Why did I receive two or three separate bills?
+
This is standard for GI procedures and is not an error. You may receive separate bills from: (1) our physician group for professional fees, (2) the facility where your procedure was performed (ambulatory surgery center or hospital), and (3) a pathology laboratory if biopsies were taken. Each is processed independently by your insurance carrier.
I was told my colonoscopy was "screening" — why was I still billed?
+
Most commercial insurance plans and Medicare cover screening colonoscopies at 100% with no patient cost-sharing. However, if a polyp is found and removed during your procedure, some insurance plans — particularly commercial plans — reclassify the visit as "diagnostic." This is called the "polyp rule" or "incidental polypectomy rule" and can result in a deductible or co-pay being applied. This varies significantly by plan. Contact your insurance carrier directly to understand your specific benefits.
What is the pathology charge on my bill?
+
If a biopsy or polyp was sent to a laboratory for microscopic analysis, you will receive a separate bill from the pathology lab — this is independent of our physician fees and the facility bill. The pathology lab submits directly to your insurance, and you are responsible for any remaining balance per your plan's terms.
What is an anesthesia or CRNA charge?
+
If your procedure was performed with monitored anesthesia care (MAC sedation or propofol), an anesthesiologist or CRNA administered your sedation. Their services are billed separately from the physician and facility. Whether this provider is in-network depends on your insurance plan — confirm anesthesia coverage with your carrier before your procedure when possible.
How do I set up a payment plan or apply for financial assistance?
+
Our billing department offers payment plans for balances over a certain threshold, and financial hardship assistance is available for qualifying patients. Please call the phone number printed on your statement to discuss options with our billing staff.
I was balance-billed by an out-of-network provider — is that allowed?
+
Federal law (the No Surprises Act, effective 2022) protects patients from surprise out-of-network bills in most emergency and many scheduled care situations. If you received an unexpected balance bill from an anesthesiologist or other provider at a procedure you scheduled in good faith at an in-network facility, you may have protections. Contact your insurance carrier or visit cms.gov/nosurprises for more information.
For account-specific billing questions, please call the number on your statement. We are unable to discuss individual account details through this website.
Condition Library

Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS)

IBS is a functional gut-brain disorder causing chronic abdominal pain, bloating, and altered bowel habits — diarrhea, constipation, or both. It is not an inflammatory condition and does not cause structural damage to the intestines or increase cancer risk. Diagnosis is based on the Rome IV criteria after ruling out organic disease.

Subtypes

IBS-D (diarrhea predominant), IBS-C (constipation predominant), IBS-M (mixed), and IBS-U (unclassified).

Common triggers

High-FODMAP foods, stress, hormonal fluctuations, antibiotic use, prior GI infection (post-infectious IBS).

Management

Low-FODMAP diet, gut-directed hypnotherapy, cognitive behavioral therapy, antispasmodics, laxatives or antidiarrheals, neuromodulators, rifaximin (IBS-D).

Alarm features to report

Rectal bleeding, unintentional weight loss, nighttime symptoms that wake you, fever, or family history of colon cancer or IBD.

Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease (GERD)

GERD occurs when stomach acid and contents reflux regularly into the esophagus, causing symptoms or mucosal damage. It is one of the most common GI diagnoses in the United States. Chronic untreated GERD can lead to esophagitis, Barrett's esophagus, and in rare cases esophageal adenocarcinoma.

Common symptoms

Heartburn, regurgitation, chest discomfort, chronic cough, hoarseness, lump-in-throat sensation, difficulty swallowing.

Lifestyle modifications

Elevate head of bed 6–8 inches, avoid eating within 3 hours of bedtime, lose weight if overweight, avoid trigger foods (caffeine, alcohol, chocolate, fatty/spicy foods, mint).

Medical management

Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), H2 blockers. Taken before meals for maximum effect. Long-term use requires periodic reassessment.

Barrett's esophagus

A complication of chronic GERD in which the esophageal lining changes to intestinal-type cells. Requires surveillance endoscopy. Rarely progresses to cancer when monitored.

Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD)

IBD encompasses Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis — chronic, relapsing-remitting inflammatory conditions of the GI tract caused by an abnormal immune response. Unlike IBS, IBD causes measurable inflammation and tissue damage and requires ongoing medical management.

Crohn's disease

Can involve any segment of the GI tract from mouth to anus. Characterized by transmural (full-thickness) inflammation, skip lesions, and risk of fistulas and strictures.

Ulcerative colitis

Involves the colon and rectum only. Continuous mucosal inflammation. Severity ranges from proctitis to pancolitis. Long-standing colitis increases colorectal cancer risk.

Treatment goals

Achieve and maintain mucosal healing (deep remission). Treatments include aminosalicylates, immunomodulators (azathioprine, methotrexate), biologics (anti-TNF, anti-integrin, IL-12/23 inhibitors), and JAK inhibitors.

Monitoring

Regular labs (CBC, CRP, fecal calprotectin), drug level monitoring, periodic endoscopic surveillance, and DEXA scans for steroid-exposed patients.

Celiac Disease

Celiac disease is an autoimmune condition in which ingestion of gluten (a protein in wheat, barley, and rye) triggers an immune response that damages the small intestinal lining (villi). The result is malabsorption of nutrients. Prevalence is approximately 1% of the population, and many cases go undiagnosed.

Symptoms

Diarrhea, bloating, fatigue, unintentional weight loss, iron-deficiency anemia, bone loss, elevated liver enzymes, infertility, dermatitis herpetiformis (skin rash).

Diagnosis

Serology (tTG-IgA, total IgA) followed by upper endoscopy with duodenal biopsies showing villous atrophy (Marsh classification). Must be tested while on a gluten-containing diet.

Treatment

Strict lifelong gluten-free diet — the only proven effective treatment. Dietitian counseling is strongly recommended. Label reading and cross-contamination awareness are critical.

Follow-up

Repeat serology at 6–12 months after starting the gluten-free diet. Check for nutritional deficiencies (iron, B12, folate, vitamin D, zinc). DEXA scan for bone density if indicated.

Small Intestinal Bacterial Overgrowth (SIBO)

SIBO occurs when bacteria that normally reside in the colon migrate into or proliferate excessively in the small intestine. This leads to fermentation of carbohydrates in the wrong location, producing gas and causing symptoms. A methane-dominant variant is now termed Intestinal Methanogen Overgrowth (IMO).

Symptoms

Bloating (often worst after meals), belching, abdominal distension, diarrhea, constipation (IMO), nausea, and in severe cases, weight loss and nutritional deficiencies.

Diagnosis

Lactulose or glucose hydrogen/methane breath test. See the Breath Testing section of this site for our FoodMarble protocol.

Treatment

Rifaximin (for hydrogen SIBO), rifaximin + neomycin (for methane/IMO), elemental diet, and dietary modification. Addressing the underlying cause (motility disorder, anatomic abnormality) is important for preventing recurrence.

Recurrence

SIBO frequently recurs without addressing predisposing factors. Prokinetics, dietary changes, and in some cases rotating antibiotic protocols are used for recurrent cases.

Eosinophilic Esophagitis (EoE)

EoE is a chronic, immune-mediated condition in which eosinophils (a type of white blood cell) accumulate in the esophageal lining, causing inflammation, tissue remodeling, and stricturing. It is increasingly recognized in both children and adults.

Symptoms

Difficulty swallowing (dysphagia), food impaction (food getting stuck), heartburn unresponsive to PPIs, chest pain, vomiting in children.

Triggers

Dietary antigens — most commonly milk, wheat, eggs, soy, tree nuts, and seafood. Environmental allergens may also contribute.

Treatment options

Topical corticosteroids (budesonide, fluticasone), proton pump inhibitors, dietary elimination (6-food, 4-food, or 2-food elimination), and biologic therapy (dupilumab) for moderate-severe disease.

Monitoring

Repeat endoscopy with biopsies is used to assess treatment response. Esophageal dilation may be needed for strictures or narrowing.

Diet & Nutrition Guidance

The low-FODMAP diet reduces fermentable carbohydrates that trigger IBS symptoms. It has the strongest evidence base of any dietary intervention for IBS, with response rates of 50–80%. It is a temporary elimination protocol followed by structured reintroduction — not a permanent diet.

Phase 1: Elimination (2–6 weeks)

Remove all high-FODMAP foods: garlic, onion, wheat, rye, lactose, apples, pears, watermelon, legumes, cashews, honey, and high-fructose corn syrup.

Phase 2: Reintroduction

Systematically reintroduce one FODMAP group at a time over 6–8 weeks to identify personal triggers. Do not skip this phase.

Phase 3: Personalization

Maintain a diet based on your individual tolerances — not a blanket low-FODMAP diet indefinitely, as this can negatively affect gut microbiome diversity.

Best resource

The Monash University FODMAP app is the gold-standard reference for current FODMAP ratings of specific foods and serving sizes.

A low-residue (low-fiber) diet is used before colonoscopy prep, during IBD flares, after bowel surgery, or when narrowing of the intestine is present.

Foods to eat

White bread, white rice, pasta, eggs, tender chicken or fish, well-cooked vegetables without skins (carrots, green beans, zucchini), canned fruit without seeds, dairy in small amounts.

Foods to avoid

Whole grains, nuts, seeds, raw vegetables, salads, corn, popcorn, dried fruit, berries, beans, lentils, tough cuts of meat, and any foods with skins or hulls.

After colonoscopic polypectomy, protecting the site of removal reduces bleeding risk. Follow these guidelines for the first 7 days.

Days 1–3: Soft diet

Eggs, yogurt, bananas, applesauce, white toast, rice, soft-cooked pasta, soup. Avoid any coarse or abrasive foods.

Avoid for 7 days

Seeds, nuts, popcorn, raw vegetables, spicy foods, NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen, aspirin). Acetaminophen (Tylenol) is safe for pain.

If you are on a blood thinner (warfarin, rivaroxaban, apixaban, clopidogrel), follow the specific restart instructions given by your physician at the time of discharge.

The gluten-free diet is the only proven treatment for celiac disease. It must be followed strictly for life — even small amounts of gluten can cause intestinal damage even without obvious symptoms.

Grains to avoid

Wheat (all forms: spelt, kamut, farro, durum, semolina), barley, rye, and triticale. Oats are safe only if certified gluten-free.

Hidden gluten sources

Soy sauce, malt vinegar, beer, many condiments, modified food starch, some medications and supplements, communion wafers, cross-contaminated oats.

Safe grains

Rice, corn, potatoes, quinoa, buckwheat, millet, certified gluten-free oats, teff, amaranth, sorghum.

Cross-contamination

Use separate cutting boards, toasters, and cooking utensils. When dining out, notify staff of celiac disease (not just a "preference") — dedicated fryers and preparation surfaces matter.

Dietary guidance on this site is general. A registered dietitian with GI specialty experience can provide individualized guidance — ask our office for a referral.
Medications Guide

Common medications prescribed in gastroenterology — what they are, what they do, and how to take them correctly.

Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) — omeprazole, pantoprazole, esomeprazole, lansoprazole
+
PPIs reduce acid production by blocking the proton pump in stomach lining cells. They are used for GERD, peptic ulcers, H. pylori treatment, and eosinophilic esophagitis. Take on an empty stomach 30–60 minutes before your first meal for maximum effectiveness. Taking with food significantly reduces efficacy. Long-term use (over 1 year) should be periodically reassessed by your physician.
Rifaximin (Xifaxan)
+
Rifaximin is a non-absorbed, gut-selective antibiotic used to treat SIBO and IBS-D (diarrhea-predominant IBS). Because it remains in the GI tract and is minimally absorbed systemically, it has fewer systemic side effects than traditional antibiotics. It is typically taken three times daily for 14 days. Take with or without food.
Mesalamine (Lialda, Asacol HD, Pentasa, Delzicol)
+
Mesalamine (5-ASA) is an anti-inflammatory medication used to induce and maintain remission in mild-to-moderate ulcerative colitis. It works locally in the colon. Formulations vary: Lialda and Asacol are designed to release in the colon, Pentasa releases throughout the small intestine and colon. Suppositories and enemas treat distal (left-sided) disease more effectively. Take oral formulations as directed — some require once daily, others are divided.
Budesonide (Entocort EC, Uceris)
+
Budesonide is a locally-acting corticosteroid with less systemic absorption than prednisone. Entocort EC is used for Crohn's disease involving the ileum and right colon. Uceris is used for mild-moderate ulcerative colitis. It is generally used for short-term induction of remission — not long-term maintenance. Side effects are fewer than systemic steroids but can still occur with prolonged use.
Biologics (infliximab, adalimumab, vedolizumab, ustekinumab, etc.)
+
Biologic therapies are targeted medicines that block specific immune pathways driving inflammation in IBD and EoE. They are given by injection or IV infusion on a scheduled basis. Before starting a biologic, tuberculosis testing, hepatitis B screening, and vaccination updates are required. Report any signs of infection (fever, cough, unusual rashes) to our office promptly while on these medications.
Linaclotide (Linzess), plecanatide (Trulance)
+
These are guanylate cyclase-C agonists used for IBS-C (constipation-predominant IBS) and chronic idiopathic constipation. They work locally in the intestine to increase fluid secretion and accelerate transit. Linzess is taken on an empty stomach 30 minutes before breakfast. Diarrhea is the most common side effect — if severe, skip a dose and contact our office.
Cholestyramine, colesevelam (bile acid sequestrants)
+
These bind bile acids in the intestine and are used for bile acid malabsorption, a common cause of chronic diarrhea — especially after cholecystectomy (gallbladder removal) or ileal disease/resection. Take separately from other medications as they can bind and reduce absorption of other drugs.
Never stop, start, or adjust a prescribed medication without consulting your physician. This guide is for general informational purposes only and does not replace the instructions provided with your prescription.
