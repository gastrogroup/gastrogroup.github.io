Colonoscopy Pre-Procedure Instructions

Please follow the instructions below prior to your colonoscopy. Also be sure to follow the instructions for your specific bowel preparation prescribed by your physician — select your prep below.

Diabetes or Blood Thinning Medications: Please contact the provider who manages these medications before your procedure and ask whether you should stop taking them and for how long prior. Do not stop blood thinners without specific guidance.

These eating and drinking instructions apply to ALL bowel preparations.

  1. 1
    2 Days Before Your Procedure
    Do not eat foods high in fiber. Examples: whole grain breads and cereals, fruits, nuts, seeds, quinoa, popcorn, and vegetables (cooked or raw).
  2. 2
    1 Day Before Your Procedure — Clear Liquid Diet Only
    Do not eat solid foods. Drink only clear liquids: water, clear fat-free broth, gelatin, clear fruit juice (apple or white grape), ice pops, carbonated beverages, sports drinks, tea, and coffee (sugar okay). Do not eat or drink dairy products.
  3. 3
    Day of Your Procedure
    Do not eat solid foods.
  4. 4
    Medications
    If you take daily medications, you may take them with sips of water before your scheduled arrival time. If you have diabetes or take blood thinners, follow instructions from the provider who manages those medications.
  5. 5
    Transportation
    Make arrangements for a responsible adult to drive you home. You may NOT leave in a taxi, rideshare, or other transportation service without a responsible adult accompanying you.
Is my prep working? Your prep is complete when your stool output is clear yellow or pale — similar to apple juice with no solid material. If you are still passing brown or cloudy material, contact our office.
Do NOT take iron for one week prior to your procedure. Notify our office if you are taking any blood thinners or GLP-1 agonists (diabetes/weight loss injectable medications such as Ozempic, Wegovy, Mounjaro).

Breath Testing — FoodMarble AIRE
  1. 1
    48 hours before: Avoid high-fiber foods, fermented foods (yogurt, kefir, sauerkraut, kombucha), and probiotics.
  2. 2
    24 hours before: Avoid complex carbohydrates — no beans, lentils, whole grains, or starchy vegetables.
  3. 3
    12 hours before: Fast completely — water only. No smoking. Avoid vigorous exercise.
  4. 4
    Morning of test: Do not brush teeth with toothpaste or use mouthwash. No probiotics. Water is fine.
  5. 5
    Baseline reading: Take a resting breath sample before consuming anything.
  6. 6
    Consume substrate: Drink the lactulose or glucose solution provided, mixed in 8 oz water within 5 minutes.
  7. 7
    Readings every 20 minutes for 3 hours. Remain seated between readings — no eating, drinking, or smoking.
  8. 8
    Upload results via FoodMarble app and share your report access code with our office.
Positive threshold: Rise of ≥20 ppm hydrogen above baseline, or ≥10 ppm methane above baseline, within the first 90 minutes.
  1. 1
    24 hours before: Avoid all dairy and fermented foods.
  2. 2
    12 hours before: Fast completely — water only.
  3. 3
    Substrate: Consume 25g lactose dissolved in 8 oz water within 5 minutes.
  4. 4
    Readings every 30 minutes for 3 hours. Record all symptoms (bloating, cramping, diarrhea).
  1. 1
    24 hours before: Avoid high-fructose fruits (apples, pears, mangoes, watermelon), honey, agave, HFCS.
  2. 2
    12 hours before: Fast completely — water only.
  3. 3
    Substrate: Consume 25g fructose dissolved in 8 oz water within 5 minutes.
  4. 4
    Readings every 30 minutes for 3 hours. Record all symptoms.

Hydrogen (H₂) — normal

Baseline under 20 ppm. Rise less than 20 ppm throughout. Bacteria are not fermenting the substrate in the small intestine.

Hydrogen — positive SIBO

Rise of ≥20 ppm above baseline within the first 90 minutes of the lactulose or glucose test.

Methane (CH₄) — IMO

Any reading ≥10 ppm above baseline suggests intestinal methanogen overgrowth, associated with constipation-predominant symptoms.

Flat-line result

If neither rises meaningfully, SIBO is unlikely. A small number of patients produce hydrogen sulfide — not measurable by standard devices — and may need further evaluation.

Results are interpreted by your physician in the context of your full clinical picture. Do not self-treat based on these results.
Understanding Your Pathology Report
Hyperplastic polyp
+
Very common and considered benign. No meaningful cancer risk. Does not typically shorten your surveillance interval.
Tubular adenoma (1–2, small)
+
Pre-cancerous but low risk when found early and completely removed. Typically leads to a 3–5 year follow-up colonoscopy recommendation.
Tubulovillous or villous adenoma
+
Higher cancer potential than tubular adenomas. Requires closer follow-up — typically 1–3 years depending on size and completeness of removal.
Low-grade vs. high-grade dysplasia
+
Dysplasia describes how abnormal cells look under a microscope. Low-grade is expected in any adenoma. High-grade carries higher risk — your physician will discuss a specific follow-up plan directly with you.
Sessile serrated lesion (SSL / SSA)
+
A distinct polyp type with its own cancer risk pathway. Guidelines generally recommend a 3-year follow-up colonoscopy, depending on size and whether dysplasia is present.
Chronic inactive gastritis / H. pylori
+
If H. pylori bacteria is identified, antibiotics plus acid suppression therapy are prescribed. A follow-up test (breath or stool antigen) is done 4–6 weeks after treatment to confirm eradication.
"Fragments of colonic mucosa, no dysplasia" — is this normal?
+
Yes — completely normal. Colon tissue was sampled and no abnormal changes were found. No further action needed.
Eosinophilic esophagitis (EoE) on biopsy
+
A chronic immune-mediated condition where eosinophils accumulate in the esophagus. Treatment may include dietary elimination, topical steroids, PPIs, or biologic therapy (dupilumab).
Your physician will contact you directly for any result requiring follow-up. Do not adjust care plans based solely on this page.

Billing & Insurance FAQ
Why did I receive two or three separate bills?
+
Standard for GI procedures. You may receive separate bills from: (1) our physician group, (2) the facility (surgery center or hospital), and (3) a pathology lab if biopsies were taken. Each is processed independently by your insurance.
My colonoscopy was "screening" — why was I billed?
+
If a polyp was removed, some insurance plans reclassify the visit as "diagnostic" — the "polyp rule." This can result in a deductible or co-pay. Contact your insurance carrier to understand your specific plan benefits.
What is the pathology charge?
+
If biopsies or polyps were sent to a lab, you will receive a separate bill from that laboratory — independent of physician and facility charges.
What is the anesthesia / CRNA charge?
+
If monitored anesthesia was used, an anesthesiologist or CRNA administered your sedation and bills separately. Confirm anesthesia coverage with your carrier before your procedure when possible.
How do I set up a payment plan?
+
Our billing department offers payment plans and financial hardship assistance. Call the number on your statement to discuss options.
What are my rights under the No Surprises Act?
+
Federal law (effective 2022) protects patients from unexpected out-of-network bills in many situations. If you received a surprise balance bill, contact your insurance or visit cms.gov/nosurprises.
For account-specific billing questions, please call the number on your statement.
Condition Library

Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS)

A functional gut-brain disorder causing chronic abdominal pain, bloating, and altered bowel habits. Not inflammatory — does not cause tissue damage or increase cancer risk. Diagnosis is based on the Rome IV criteria.

Subtypes

IBS-D (diarrhea), IBS-C (constipation), IBS-M (mixed), IBS-U (unclassified).

Common triggers

High-FODMAP foods, stress, hormonal changes, antibiotics, prior GI infection.

Management

Low-FODMAP diet, gut-directed therapy, antispasmodics, rifaximin (IBS-D), linaclotide (IBS-C).

Alarm features to report

Rectal bleeding, weight loss, nighttime symptoms, fever, family history of colon cancer or IBD.

GERD

Stomach acid regularly refluxes into the esophagus causing symptoms or mucosal damage. Chronic untreated GERD can lead to esophagitis, Barrett's esophagus, or rarely esophageal cancer.

Common symptoms

Heartburn, regurgitation, chronic cough, hoarseness, difficulty swallowing.

Lifestyle changes

Elevate head of bed 6–8 inches, avoid eating 3 hours before bed, lose weight, avoid caffeine, alcohol, chocolate, fatty/spicy foods.

Medical treatment

PPIs, H2 blockers. Take on an empty stomach before meals for maximum effect.

Barrett's esophagus

Complication of chronic GERD — esophageal lining changes to intestinal-type cells. Requires surveillance endoscopy.

Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD)

Includes Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis — chronic, immune-mediated conditions causing GI inflammation and tissue damage. Requires ongoing medical management.

Crohn's disease

Can involve any GI segment. Full-thickness inflammation, skip lesions, risk of fistulas and strictures.

Ulcerative colitis

Involves colon and rectum only. Continuous mucosal inflammation. Long-standing colitis increases colorectal cancer risk.

Treatment

Aminosalicylates, immunomodulators, biologics (anti-TNF, anti-integrin, IL-12/23 inhibitors), JAK inhibitors.

Monitoring

Regular labs, drug levels, fecal calprotectin, periodic endoscopic surveillance.

Celiac Disease

An autoimmune condition triggered by gluten that damages the small intestinal lining, causing malabsorption. Affects approximately 1% of the population — many cases go undiagnosed.

Symptoms

Diarrhea, fatigue, weight loss, iron-deficiency anemia, bone loss, elevated liver enzymes, skin rash (dermatitis herpetiformis).

Diagnosis

Serology (tTG-IgA) followed by upper endoscopy with duodenal biopsies. Must be tested while on a gluten-containing diet.

Treatment

Strict lifelong gluten-free diet — the only proven treatment. Dietitian counseling strongly recommended.

Follow-up

Repeat serology at 6–12 months. Check for iron, B12, folate, vitamin D, zinc deficiencies. DEXA scan if indicated.

SIBO / IMO

Small intestinal bacterial overgrowth occurs when colonic bacteria proliferate in the small intestine, causing fermentation, gas, and symptoms. Methane-dominant variant is termed IMO (intestinal methanogen overgrowth).

Symptoms

Bloating after meals, belching, distension, diarrhea or constipation (IMO), nausea.

Diagnosis

Lactulose or glucose hydrogen/methane breath test. See the Breath Testing section for our FoodMarble protocol.

Treatment

Rifaximin (hydrogen SIBO), rifaximin + neomycin (IMO), elemental diet, dietary modification.

Recurrence

SIBO frequently recurs. Prokinetics and addressing underlying causes are key for prevention.

Eosinophilic Esophagitis (EoE)

A chronic immune-mediated condition where eosinophils accumulate in the esophageal lining, causing inflammation, remodeling, and narrowing.

Symptoms

Difficulty swallowing, food impaction, heartburn unresponsive to PPIs, chest pain.

Common food triggers

Milk, wheat, eggs, soy, tree nuts, seafood.

Treatment

Topical corticosteroids, PPIs, dietary elimination, dupilumab (biologic) for moderate-severe disease.

Monitoring

Repeat endoscopy with biopsies assesses treatment response. Dilation for strictures if needed.

Condition descriptions are educational overviews. Your physician will tailor diagnosis and treatment to your specific situation.
Diet & Nutrition Guidance

The low-FODMAP diet reduces fermentable carbohydrates that trigger IBS symptoms. It is a temporary elimination protocol — not a permanent diet — followed by structured reintroduction.

Phase 1: Elimination (2–6 weeks)

Remove all high-FODMAP foods: garlic, onion, wheat, rye, lactose, apples, pears, legumes, cashews, honey, HFCS.

Phase 2: Reintroduction

Reintroduce one FODMAP group at a time over 6–8 weeks to identify personal triggers. Do not skip this phase.

Phase 3: Personalization

Maintain a diet based on individual tolerances — not a blanket low-FODMAP diet indefinitely.

Best resource

The Monash University FODMAP app is the gold-standard for current FODMAP ratings of specific foods and serving sizes.

✅ Foods to eat

White bread, white rice, pasta, eggs, tender chicken or fish, well-cooked vegetables without skins, canned fruit without seeds, dairy in small amounts.

❌ Foods to avoid

Whole grains, nuts, seeds, raw vegetables, corn, popcorn, dried fruit, berries, beans, lentils, tough meats, foods with skins or hulls.

Days 1–3: Soft diet

Eggs, yogurt, bananas, applesauce, white toast, rice, soft pasta, soup.

Avoid for 7 days

Seeds, nuts, popcorn, raw vegetables, spicy foods, NSAIDs, aspirin. Tylenol is safe for pain.

If you are on a blood thinner, follow the specific restart instructions given at discharge.

Grains to avoid

Wheat (all forms: spelt, kamut, farro, durum, semolina), barley, rye, triticale. Oats only if certified gluten-free.

Hidden gluten

Soy sauce, malt vinegar, beer, many condiments, modified food starch, some medications and supplements.

Safe grains

Rice, corn, potatoes, quinoa, buckwheat, millet, certified GF oats, teff, amaranth, sorghum.

Cross-contamination

Use separate cutting boards and toasters. When dining out, specify celiac disease — dedicated fryers and prep surfaces matter.

A registered dietitian with GI specialty can provide individualized guidance — ask our office for a referral.
Medications Guide
Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) — omeprazole, pantoprazole, esomeprazole
+
PPIs reduce acid production in the stomach. Used for GERD, ulcers, H. pylori treatment, and EoE. Take on an empty stomach 30–60 minutes before your first meal. Long-term use requires periodic reassessment.
Rifaximin (Xifaxan)
+
A non-absorbed, gut-selective antibiotic for SIBO and IBS-D. Stays in the GI tract with minimal systemic absorption. Typically taken three times daily for 14 days, with or without food.
Mesalamine (Lialda, Asacol HD, Pentasa)
+
Anti-inflammatory medication for mild-to-moderate ulcerative colitis. Works locally in the colon. Available as oral tablets, suppositories, and enemas. Take as directed — formulations vary in dosing schedule.
Budesonide (Entocort EC, Uceris)
+
Locally-acting corticosteroid with less systemic absorption than prednisone. Entocort EC for Crohn's involving the ileum/right colon. Uceris for ulcerative colitis. Used short-term for induction of remission.
Biologics (infliximab, adalimumab, vedolizumab, ustekinumab)
+
Targeted therapies that block specific immune pathways in IBD and EoE. Given by injection or IV infusion on a schedule. TB testing, hepatitis B screening, and vaccine updates required before starting. Report any signs of infection promptly.
Linaclotide (Linzess) / Plecanatide (Trulance)
+
Used for IBS-C and chronic constipation. Work locally to increase intestinal fluid and accelerate transit. Linzess taken on an empty stomach 30 minutes before breakfast. Diarrhea is the most common side effect.
Never stop, start, or adjust a prescribed medication without consulting your physician.
Hemorrhoid Banding (Rubber Band Ligation)

Rubber band ligation is the most effective office-based treatment for internal hemorrhoids (grades I–III). A small elastic band is placed around the base of the hemorrhoid, cutting off its blood supply. The hemorrhoid shrinks and falls off within 7–10 days.

Who is it for?

Internal hemorrhoids causing bleeding, prolapse, or discomfort that has not responded to dietary and lifestyle changes.

Success rate

Approximately 70–80% effective per session. Most patients require 2–3 sessions spaced 4–6 weeks apart.

What it does NOT treat

External hemorrhoids (outside the anal opening) cannot be banded. Surgical hemorrhoidectomy may be needed for large external or mixed hemorrhoids.

Duration

The banding itself takes only a few minutes and is typically performed in the office during a colonoscopy or flexible sigmoidoscopy visit.

  1. 1
    If banding is performed at the time of a colonoscopy, follow your standard bowel prep instructions.
  2. 2
    If performed as a standalone office procedure, a Fleet enema the morning of the visit is typically sufficient. Follow the specific instructions given at scheduling.
  3. 3
    Blood thinners: Inform our office of all blood-thinning medications. You may need to hold these before the procedure.
  4. 4
    Eat a light meal beforehand unless instructed otherwise. No sedation is typically required for in-office banding.
  1. 1
    Diet: Increase fiber intake (25–35g per day) and fluid intake. Soft, regular stools reduce straining and promote healing. A stool softener (docusate) may be recommended.
  2. 2
    Activity: Avoid heavy lifting, straining, and strenuous exercise for 48–72 hours.
  3. 3
    Pain management: Mild discomfort and pressure for 1–3 days is normal. Tylenol is appropriate. Avoid NSAIDs for at least 7 days.
  4. 4
    Band passage: The band and hemorrhoid tissue fall off within 7–10 days. A small amount of blood or tissue in the toilet at that time is normal.
  5. 5
    Sitz baths: Sitting in warm water for 10–15 minutes 2–3 times per day can relieve discomfort.
  6. 6
    Avoid blood thinners for 7–10 days post-procedure unless medically necessary — confirm with your physician.
Call our office immediately or go to the ER if you experience: heavy rectal bleeding (soaking multiple pads), fever above 101°F, severe pain unrelieved by Tylenol, difficulty urinating, or signs of infection.

During the procedure

You will be positioned on your side. A small anoscope is inserted to visualize the hemorrhoid. The band placement takes seconds — you may feel pressure but typically not sharp pain.

Immediately after

A sensation of fullness, pressure, or mild aching is normal for 24–48 hours. Most patients return to normal activity the same day.

Days 2–10

The banded tissue gradually shrinks and falls off. Some spotting of blood is expected. Avoid constipation — straining increases bleeding risk.

Follow-up

A follow-up visit is scheduled 4–6 weeks later. If multiple hemorrhoids are present, additional banding sessions are performed at these visits.

Follow all specific instructions provided by your physician at the time of your procedure.
Capsule Endoscopy Prep Guide

Capsule endoscopy (the "pill camera") allows your doctor to examine the entire small intestine — a region standard endoscopy and colonoscopy cannot reach. You swallow a vitamin-sized capsule containing a tiny camera that takes thousands of photos as it passes naturally through your digestive tract.

Why it's ordered

Obscure GI bleeding, unexplained iron-deficiency anemia, Crohn's disease evaluation, small bowel tumors, celiac disease assessment.

How long?

You wear a recording device for approximately 8–10 hours. The capsule passes naturally in a bowel movement within 24–48 hours.

Is it safe?

Yes for most patients. If you have known bowel narrowing (stricture), swallowing difficulties, or a pacemaker, notify our office before the test.

Do I need sedation?

No — you simply swallow the capsule with water. Most patients go about their normal day during the recording period.

  1. 1
    3–5 days before: Stop iron supplements — iron can coat the bowel lining and interfere with image quality.
  2. 2
    Day before: Eat a low-fiber, low-residue diet. Avoid red or orange colored foods and drinks.
  3. 3
    Day before after 9:00 PM: Nothing to eat. Clear liquids only (water, white grape juice, plain broth).
  4. 4
    Bowel prep (if prescribed): Some physicians prescribe a prep to improve small bowel visibility. Follow your specific instructions if a prep was ordered.
  5. 5
    Medications: Take essential morning medications with a small sip of water at least 1 hour before swallowing the capsule. Hold GLP-1 medications (Ozempic, Wegovy) — contact our office for guidance.
If you have a cardiac pacemaker, ICD, or neurostimulator, notify our office immediately — special precautions or device compatibility checks are required.
  1. 1
    Arrive at the designated location. The sensor belt or vest will be fitted to your abdomen.
  2. 2
    Swallow the capsule with a small amount of water.
  3. 3
    First 2 hours: Nothing to eat or drink. Remain upright (sitting or walking).
  4. 4
    After 2 hours: You may drink clear liquids.
  5. 5
    After 4 hours: You may eat a light snack (crackers, toast).
  6. 6
    Avoid MRI scans and strong magnetic fields during the recording period. Avoid strenuous exercise and repeated bending.
  7. 7
    Return the recording device at the end of the recording period as instructed.
  1. 1
    Resume normal diet and activities once the recording device is returned.
  2. 2
    The capsule passes naturally within 24–72 hours. You do not need to retrieve it.
  3. 3
    Your physician will review the images (this takes several days) and contact you with results.
  4. 4
    If you do not pass the capsule within 2 weeks, contact our office. Capsule retention is rare but can occur with strictures.
Call our office or go to the ER if you experience: difficulty swallowing after taking the capsule, significant abdominal pain or distension, nausea, or vomiting.
Always follow the specific instructions provided by our office at scheduling.
FibroScan® — Liver Stiffness Testing

FibroScan® is a non-invasive, painless ultrasound-based test that measures liver stiffness (fibrosis) and fat content (steatosis). It is used as an alternative to liver biopsy to assess liver damage in NAFLD/MASLD, hepatitis B and C, alcoholic liver disease, and other chronic liver conditions.

Why it's ordered

To assess liver fibrosis stage, monitor disease progression, evaluate fatty liver disease (NAFLD/MASLD), or follow up after hepatitis treatment.

How it works

A probe placed on the skin over the liver sends a mild vibration and ultrasound wave. The speed of the wave indicates liver stiffness. Takes 5–10 minutes.

Is it painful?

No — completely painless. You will feel only a mild vibration on the right side of your abdomen. No needles, no sedation, no radiation.

Limitations

Results may be less reliable with very high BMI, active hepatitis inflammation, or significant ascites. Your physician will factor this into interpretation.

  1. 1
    Fast for at least 2–3 hours before the test. Eating causes temporary changes in liver blood flow that can affect results. Water is fine.
  2. 2
    Avoid alcohol for at least 24 hours before your appointment.
  3. 3
    Wear comfortable, loose-fitting clothing that allows easy access to the right side of your abdomen.
  4. 4
    Continue all regular medications unless instructed otherwise.
FibroScan requires no bowel prep, sedation, or IV access. You can drive yourself and return to normal activity immediately after.

FibroScan measures two values — liver stiffness (kPa, reflecting fibrosis) and CAP score (dB/m, reflecting fat content). Results are interpreted by your physician alongside your labs and clinical history.

Liver stiffness (kPa)

Lower values (under 7 kPa) generally suggest minimal fibrosis. Higher values suggest advancing fibrosis or cirrhosis. Your physician will discuss your specific result in context.

CAP score — fat content

Measures hepatic steatosis (liver fat). Higher scores indicate greater fat accumulation. Used to grade and monitor fatty liver disease.

What happens next?

Depending on results, your physician may recommend lifestyle changes, medication, additional imaging (MRI), or in rare cases, liver biopsy for definitive staging.

Can it replace biopsy?

In many cases, yes. FibroScan reduces the need for invasive biopsy. However, biopsy may still be needed when results are inconclusive.

FibroScan results are interpreted by your physician together with your labs, symptoms, and medical history. Do not interpret results in isolation.

Endoscopic Ultrasound (EUS)

Endoscopic ultrasound combines an endoscope with a high-frequency ultrasound probe to produce detailed images of the GI tract walls and nearby organs (pancreas, liver, bile ducts, lymph nodes). It can also obtain tissue samples (FNA/FNB) from deep structures.

Why it's ordered

Pancreatic cysts or masses, pancreatitis, bile duct stones, submucosal GI tumors, staging of GI cancers, unexplained abdominal pain.

Prep (upper EUS)

Same as upper endoscopy — nothing to eat or drink for 6–8 hours. IV sedation is used. Arrange a driver.

Prep (lower EUS)

If examining the rectum or lower GI tract, a bowel prep or enema may be required. Follow specific instructions provided.

If a biopsy is taken

Results return in 5–7 business days. Blood thinners may need to be held for 5–7 days after FNA/FNB.

EUS is a specialized procedure. Follow all instructions provided at scheduling.
ERCP — Bile Duct & Pancreas Procedure

ERCP (Endoscopic Retrograde Cholangiopancreatography) combines endoscopy and X-ray imaging to diagnose and treat problems in the bile ducts and pancreatic duct. It is used to remove bile duct stones, treat blockages, place stents, or evaluate unexplained jaundice.

Why it's ordered

Bile duct stones, strictures, pancreatic duct problems, jaundice, recurrent pancreatitis, or biliary leaks after surgery.

Prep

Nothing to eat or drink for 6–8 hours. IV sedation or general anesthesia is used. You will need a driver — do not drive for 24 hours.

Blood thinners

Most blood thinners must be held before ERCP. Your physician will provide specific instructions based on your medications.

After the procedure

Mild sore throat and bloating are common. Eat light foods several hours after. Watch for fever, severe abdominal pain, or jaundice — these require immediate attention.

Post-ERCP pancreatitis occurs in approximately 3–5% of cases. Symptoms include worsening abdominal pain radiating to the back, nausea, and vomiting within 24 hours. Go to the ER if these occur.
ERCP is a specialized procedure. All prep instructions will be provided at scheduling.
